A stone in the common bile duct is choledocholithiasis; infection of the biliary system is acute cholangitis. They can occur together, but identifying a stone and recognizing an infected obstructed system are separate diagnostic tasks.

## Clinical overview

Choledocholithiasis may cause biliary pain, jaundice, pancreatitis, or no obvious symptoms. Cholangitis can progress to organ dysfunction and requires prompt clinical assessment. Fever, right upper quadrant pain, and jaundice form the classic Charcot triad, but its absence does not exclude the disease. I understand the triad as a recognizable presentation rather than a checklist that must be completed before treatment begins.

The [Tokyo Guidelines 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29032610/) organize diagnosis around systemic inflammation, cholestasis, and imaging evidence of biliary dilatation or an underlying cause. This framework matters because ultrasound cannot directly establish the full infectious syndrome. Laboratory findings, vital signs, and clinical course contribute information that images do not contain.

## Anatomy and pathophysiology

Duct stones commonly migrate from the gallbladder, although stones can also form within the biliary tree. Their effect depends on location and whether obstruction is persistent or intermittent. A stone near the ampulla may interfere with biliary drainage and can also be associated with pancreatitis. A patient may therefore have several related processes rather than one mutually exclusive diagnosis.

In cholangitis, impaired drainage facilitates infection and increased biliary pressure can contribute to systemic spread. Stones are one cause, but malignant or benign strictures and obstructed stents can produce the same clinical emergency. Duct diameter and stone size do not establish infection severity.

## Diagnostic workflow and imaging findings

### Assess illness severity before completing the image workup

Initial assessment includes vital signs, mental status, evidence of organ dysfunction, and laboratory tests for inflammation, cholestasis, and relevant complications. Blood cultures can help guide treatment when obtained without delaying necessary antibiotics. The [Tokyo initial-management guidance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28941329/) emphasizes beginning urgent supportive treatment before a definitive diagnosis when the patient is unstable. A pending scan should not defer that assessment.

### Look for a duct stone and secondary signs

On ultrasound, a duct stone may appear as an echogenic intraductal focus with posterior shadowing. Small stones may not shadow clearly. Upstream dilatation supports impaired drainage, but a nondilated duct does not exclude a small, early, or intermittently obstructing stone. The distal common bile duct is often incompletely visualized. Gallbladder stones increase suspicion of migration but do not prove that a duct stone is currently present.

### Resolve an intermediate likelihood with EUS or MRCP

When initial findings leave a meaningful possibility of choledocholithiasis, endoscopic ultrasound or MRCP can provide further evidence. The [ASGE guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30979521/) recommends either approach for intermediate-risk patients, with choice influenced by availability, expertise, and patient factors. EUS can be useful for small distal stones; MRCP provides a noninvasive ductal map. A negative result still needs interpretation against persistent symptoms and the quality of the examination.

### Distinguish diagnostic uncertainty from a drainage indication

ASGE directs high-risk presentations, including ascending cholangitis or a demonstrated duct stone, toward ERCP rather than repeated purely diagnostic testing. ERCP can decompress the system and remove stones, but it introduces procedural risks. In an unstable patient, the immediate goal may be drainage, with definitive stone treatment planned according to clinical status. The timing and extent of intervention therefore answer a different question from whether a stone is visible.

## Differential diagnosis and management context

Acute cholecystitis, pancreatitis, hepatitis, and nonbiliary sources of sepsis can overlap clinically. Cholangitis also occurs without stones, so an empty-looking duct does not exclude an infected stricture or blocked stent. Tokyo guidance links drainage to severity and treatment response: mild cases may initially respond to medical treatment, while moderate and severe disease require more urgent source-control planning. Antimicrobials, resuscitation, and biliary drainage are complementary components of care.

## Implications for medical AI

I read this distinction as a limit on image-only labeling. “Duct stone present” can be anchored to imaging or extraction, whereas “cholangitis” requires clinical evidence and a defined diagnostic framework. Using the latter as an image label may reward associations with severe illness or prior intervention rather than evidence of infection.

For my hepatobiliary work, I would record whether imaging preceded antibiotics, drainage, or stone passage. A stone absent at later ERCP does not automatically invalidate an earlier image finding. This suggests that audit cases need a timeline, including which evidence was available when the prediction would have been used.
