Biliary obstruction is impaired bile flow caused by a blockage or narrowing along the drainage pathway. Duct dilatation is an important clue, but the examination must also establish the likely level, cause, and clinical consequences.

## Clinical overview

Patients may present with jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, pruritus, or abdominal pain. Some abnormalities are detected during investigation of liver tests or unrelated imaging. A cholestatic biochemical pattern supports impaired bile handling, but does not by itself distinguish mechanical obstruction from intrahepatic cholestasis. I keep that distinction explicit because an abnormal laboratory result and a dilated duct answer different questions.

Fever, hypotension, or altered mental status changes the urgency by raising concern for biliary infection and systemic illness. Conversely, painless jaundice can prompt investigation for malignancy but is not a diagnosis of cancer. The clinical context determines how quickly the anatomical uncertainty must be resolved.

## Anatomy and pathophysiology

Bile passes from small intrahepatic ducts through the hepatic ducts and extrahepatic biliary tree into the duodenum. A distal blockage can affect both intrahepatic and extrahepatic ducts; a hilar or segmental lesion may dilate only the ducts upstream from that location. Stones, ductal tumors, postoperative scarring, and external compression produce different mechanisms of impaired drainage.

Dilatation reflects the interaction between obstruction, its duration, and the duct's capacity to expand. Early or intermittent obstruction may occur without conspicuous dilatation, while enlargement can persist after obstruction resolves. Age and previous cholecystectomy also affect duct caliber. [Lucius and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3390/diagnostics15070919) review these influences, which make one universal diameter cutoff inadequate for every patient.

## Diagnostic workflow and imaging findings

### Begin with the clinical question

The referral should establish symptoms, biochemical abnormalities, previous biliary procedures, and whether infection is suspected. Ultrasound is commonly an initial examination because it can assess ducts, gallstones, liver parenchyma, and some obstructing lesions. The [ACR jaundice guidance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31054739/) frames imaging selection around the suspected mechanism. I would distinguish “look for mechanical obstruction” from “explain jaundice,” since the latter includes conditions without a blocked duct.

### Confirm and describe duct dilatation

Dilated intrahepatic ducts appear as branching fluid-filled structures alongside portal vessels. Color Doppler helps distinguish ducts from vessels, provided settings can detect the relevant flow. The extrahepatic duct should be measured perpendicular to its axis, with the measurement site documented. Distribution matters: diffuse, unilateral, or segmental dilatation can be more informative than the largest measurement. Limited visualization should remain visible in the report.

### Locate the transition and search for a cause

The examination follows dilated ducts toward a narrowing, abrupt cutoff, stone, or mass. Distal obstruction may be difficult to assess because bowel gas obscures the pancreatic head and terminal duct. A visible stone is direct evidence; upstream dilatation without a visible lesion is indirect evidence. The gallbladder, pancreatic region, and porta hepatis should be assessed together. An enlarged gallbladder alone cannot identify the cause or reliably separate benign from malignant obstruction.

### Choose the next modality deliberately

MRCP uses fluid-sensitive MRI sequences to map the biliary tree without requiring duct cannulation. It can show the distribution of narrowing and filling defects, but a filling defect is not automatically a stone: air, debris, and artifacts can complicate interpretation. [Griffin and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22695995/) describe these pitfalls. Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI can better assess a suspected mass and surrounding tissues. Endoscopic ultrasound may clarify distal duct or periampullary abnormalities.

## Differential diagnosis and management context

Nonobstructive duct enlargement, hepatitis, medication-related cholestasis, and other intrahepatic disorders remain relevant when imaging and biochemistry disagree. A technically limited ultrasound cannot exclude obstruction, while mild isolated enlargement does not automatically justify intervention. ERCP is generally used when drainage, stone extraction, or tissue acquisition is needed. Urgent infection management and planned evaluation of a stable stricture follow different pathways; diagnostic imaging should not delay necessary treatment of sepsis.

## Implications for medical AI

I read this workflow as a hierarchy of targets: detect dilatation, localize obstruction, identify its cause, and assess urgency. Success at the first task does not establish competence at the others. A model could recognize enlarged ducts while remaining unable to distinguish a recently relieved obstruction from an active blockage.

For gallbladder ultrasound and clinical faithfulness auditing, I would retain duct visibility, measurement location, prior procedures, and the timing of laboratory results. This suggests evaluating direct lesion evidence separately from secondary signs. A prediction based on duct enlargement may be clinically reasonable, but its explanation should not claim that the obstructing lesion was visualized when it was not.
